# Track 2: Narrative, coherence and explanatory writing
## Sources
- [2.1] Jerome Bruner, Actual Minds, Possible Worlds: the two modes of thought (The Marginalian) — https://www.themarginalian.org/2016/01/20/jerome-bruner-actual-minds-possible-worlds-storytelling/ — blog — Bruner separates paradigmatic thought (truth, categories, context-free) from narrative thought (meaning, particulars, temporal), so sard should pick the mode that fits the material and not force a story onto paradigmatic content such as proofs or taxonomies.
- [2.2] Daniel Willingham, "Ask the Cognitive Scientist: The Privileged Status of Story" (American Educator, Summer 2004) — https://www.aft.org/periodical/american-educator/summer-2004/ask-cognitive-scientist — paper — Stories run on the "4 Cs" (causality, conflict, complications, character), and conflict is the part teachers should reuse: open with a compelling problem whose solution becomes the lesson. sard has causality but no explicit conflict/problem slot. Willingham also warns that stories should support deep engagement with the subject, not replace it.
- [2.3] Mar, Li, Nguyen & Ta (2021), "Memory and comprehension of narrative versus expository texts: A meta-analysis" — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8219577/ — paper — 75+ samples, 33k participants: narratives beat expository texts (g≈.55; memory g≈.72, comprehension g≈.48). The authors attribute the gain to familiar structure, warn against forcing all information into narrative form, and report publication bias. This is evidence for sard's core and also a limit on it.
- [2.4] Mensink, Kendeou & Rapp (2021), "Do different kinds of introductions influence comprehension and memory for scientific explanations?" — https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/do-different-kinds-of-introductions-influence-comprehension-and-m/ — paper — Any introduction improved recall of scientific explanations. Narrative introductions were remembered better but slowed later processing, and the effects "not always align with popular instructional prescriptions". sard's "foundation" is supported, but narrative hooks are not free.
- [2.5] Dahlstrom (2014), "Using narratives and storytelling to communicate science with nonexpert audiences" (PNAS) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4183170 — paper — Narrative increases comprehension, interest and engagement for non-experts, but it is intrinsically persuasive and raises ethical questions, so sard must keep explaining separate from persuading.
- [2.6] Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Understanding" (Grimm) — https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/understanding/ — encyclopedia — Understanding means grasping explanatory and dependence relations among pieces of information, which isolated facts do not give. This is the philosophical basis for sard's insistence on connection over listing.
- [2.7] Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Philosophy of History" — https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/history/ — encyclopedia — Colligation (placing an event in the larger process where it makes sense) is what sard-roots does. Selection means there can be "multiple truthful, unbiased, and inconsistent narratives" of the same events, so a sard narrative is one honest path, not the only one.
- [2.8] Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Causal Approaches to Scientific Explanation" (Woodward & Ross) — https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/causal-explanation-science/ — encyclopedia — A good causal explanation answers "what-if-things-had-been-different" questions and includes only the factors that make a difference. This gives sard a test for its stations and for "why is it this way and not otherwise" in sard-roots.
- [2.9] "What did Danto show?" (on Arthur Danto's narrative sentences) — https://philsci-archive.pitt.edu/29862/1/Danto%2011.docx — paper — Narrative sentences describe an earlier event in light of later ones, information not available at the time. sard's "arrival" is written with hindsight and must not make outcomes look inevitable.
- [2.10] Rhetorical Structure Theory (Wikipedia) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhetorical_structure_theory — encyclopedia — RST models a coherent text as a tree of about 25 relations, each linking a nucleus (the core claim) to a satellite (evidence, background, concession, and so on). Satellites can be deleted and the text still reads, while nuclei cannot. This suggests putting the nucleus right after the bold connector, and it offers a richer relation inventory than "therefore".
- [2.11] Sanders, Spooren & Noordman (1992), "Toward a taxonomy of coherence relations" — https://dspace.library.uu.nl/handle/1874/295270 — paper — Coherence relations break down into four cognitive primitives: causal vs additive, semantic vs pragmatic, positive vs negative, basic vs non-basic order. This is a compact and psychologically plausible checklist for verifying that each sard connector is "logically true".
- [2.12] Degand & Sanders (2002), "The impact of relational markers on expository text comprehension in L1 and L2" — https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/the-impact-of-relational-markers-on-expository-text-comprehension/ — paper — Causal connectives improved comprehension of expository texts in both first-language and second-language reading. This supports explicit connectors when sard explains a source written in a language other than the reader's.
- [2.13] "Interaction between connectives and previous knowledge in the processing of causal coherence" (CLAC) — https://revistas.ucm.es/index.php/CLAC/en/article/view/62504 — paper — When readers lack domain knowledge, connectives can be what makes comprehension possible at all. They increase reading time but deepen understanding. Novices need more explicit markers.
- [2.14] McNamara et al. (1996) reverse cohesion effect, summarized by BYU Editing Research — https://editingresearch.byu.edu/2022/11/23/how-less-clarity-could-improve-active-processing — blog — Highly cohesive text helps low-knowledge readers. High-knowledge readers build a deeper situation model from less cohesive text that makes them infer the links themselves. sard's connector density should therefore depend on the audience.
- [2.15] Kintsch (2005), "An overview of top-down and bottom-up effects in comprehension: The CI perspective" — https://condor.depaul.edu/~dallbrit/extra/hon207/readings/kintsch-2005-overview-of-top-down-and-bottom-up-effects-CI.pdf — paper — Comprehension builds three levels: surface code, textbase, and the situation model formed by fusing the text with prior knowledge. sard's "from fact to meaning" rule aims at the situation model, and the foundation supplies the prior knowledge that model needs.
- [2.16] "Children's and adults' memory for television stories: the role of causal factors" (Univ. of Kentucky scholars record) — https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/childrens-and-adults-memory-for-television-stories-the-role-of-ca/ — paper — In the causal-network tradition (Trabasso and van den Broek), events on the causal chain are recalled more, summarized more and judged more important than dead ends. sard should prune or flag stations that do not lead to the arrival.
- [2.17] Lorch et al., "Signaling topic structure via headings or preview sentences" — https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/signaling-topic-structure-via-headings-or-preview-sentences/ — paper — Signals improve memory for what they cue and leave unsignaled content unaffected. Headings beat preview sentences for remembering topic structure. sard's bold connector should cue the station's key idea, and long outputs may need headings.
- [2.18] "Thirty years after Ausubel: An updated meta-analysis of advance organizer research" — https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/20405 — paper — Advance organizers produced generally positive effects, strongest for weaker or younger learners. sard's "foundation" is in effect an advance organizer and should give a conceptual frame, not only definitions.
- [2.19] "Cut It Out: Learning with Seductive Details" (The Learning Scientists) — https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2019/6/20-1 — blog — Interesting but irrelevant details hurt recall and transfer through overload and schema interference. This is a direct risk for sard-roots and sard-book anecdotes, which are colorful but off the main line.
- [2.20] "The Curse of Knowledge: Pinker Describes a Key Cause of Bad Writing" (APS Observer) — https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/the-curse-of-knowledge-pinker-describes-a-key-cause-of-bad-writing — news — Experts skip jargon definitions and leave logic unstated. Pinker's remedies are concrete examples instead of abstractions, classic style (show the reader the thing), and testing drafts on representative readers.
- [2.21] Curse of knowledge (Wikipedia) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curse_of_knowledge — encyclopedia — The term comes from Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber (1989), and Newton's tapper/listener study is the classic demonstration. Informed people cannot simulate an uninformed mind, which is why sard needs an explicit "what does this reader not know?" step.
- [2.22] Review of Made to Stick (Heath & Heath), Stanford Social Innovation Review — https://ssir.org/books/reviews/entry/review_made_to_stick — book — SUCCESs (Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Stories) together with the curse of knowledge. "Concrete" and "Unexpected" are the parts that transfer to sard: anchor abstract stations in a concrete case and expose the gap the reader did not expect.
- [2.23] SCQA Logic (Minto's Pyramid Principle), ThinkInsights — https://thinkinsights.net/strategy/scqa-logic — blog — Situation, Complication, Question, Answer, then the governing answer first with support below. This is a top-down alternative for decision-makers. Its SCQ opening is itself a mini-narrative and fits sard's foundation.
- [2.24] Inverted pyramid (journalism), Wikipedia — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverted_pyramid_(journalism) — encyclopedia — Most important information first, so readers can stop at any point. This is the strongest case against delaying the meaning to the end, and it supports a mandatory compass on long outputs.
- [2.25] SESYNC Communications Toolkit: And, But, Therefore statement (Randy Olson's ABT) — https://www.sesync.org/resources/communications-toolkit-and-therefore-statement — docs — The pattern is "And" (context), "But" (problem), "Therefore" (resolution). A plain "and… and…" sequence is a list pretending to be a story, so sard needs at least one real "But" (tension) per explanation.
- [2.26] Olah & Carter, "Research Debt" (Distill, 2017) — https://distill.pub/2017/research-debt/ — blog — Poor exposition, undigested ideas and bad notation are all forms of debt. Distillation means "transforming the idea" through better analogies and abstractions, not polishing prose. sard should sometimes reframe an idea, not only reorder it.
- [2.27] Paul Graham, "How to Write Usefully" — https://paulgraham.com/useful.html — blog — Usefulness comes from importance × novelty × correctness × strength. Qualifications express a degree of certainty and are a skill, not a weakness. sard needs calibrated hedging, especially in "a note of my own".
- [2.28] Paul Graham, "Putting Ideas into Words" — https://paulgraham.com/words.html — blog — Writing about something reveals that you did not understand it as well as you thought. When sard cannot write an honest connector between two stations, it has found a gap and should report it, not cover it.
- [2.29] Columbia Journalism Review on Vox's explanatory-journalism design (card stacks) — https://www.cjr.org/?p=100714 — news — Vox splits explanation into a linear article plus modular, continuously updated Q&A "cards". Narrative and reference serve different needs, which is relevant to sard-deck study decks and to reuse.
- [2.30] Narrative fallacy (LessWrong wiki, after Taleb) — https://www.lesswrong.com/w/narrative-fallacy — blog — People weave explanations into sequences of facts and force "an arrow of relationship" onto them, which inflates felt understanding. Suggested remedies: write the "anti-story" and distinguish systems from stories.
- [2.31] Conjunction fallacy (Wikipedia) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjunction_fallacy — encyclopedia — Each added specific detail makes a scenario feel more plausible while making it less probable. Vivid details invented by the explainer make a sard narrative more convincing and less true.
- [2.32] Lombrozo (2007), "Simplicity and probability in causal explanation" (Cognitive Psychology) — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17097080/ — paper — People prefer explanations with fewer causes and give them inflated prior probability. A neat single-cause chain is attractive even when the source describes several causes.
- [2.33] Illusion of explanatory depth (Wikipedia; Rozenblit & Keil 2002) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusion_of_explanatory_depth — encyclopedia — Felt understanding of causal mechanisms collapses when people try to explain them. The illusion is strongest for explanatory knowledge and weaker for narratives or facts, so a smooth narrative can leave a reader overconfident unless they are prompted to explain it back.
- [2.34] Transportation theory (psychology), Wikipedia (Green & Brock 2000) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transportation_theory_(psychology) — encyclopedia — Readers absorbed in a story counterargue less and change beliefs more. When sard narrates someone's argument (sard-article), the smooth retelling itself can make the argument seem stronger than it is.
- [2.35] Narrative paradigm (Walter Fisher), Wikipedia — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narrative_paradigm — encyclopedia — Fisher judges a story by two criteria: coherence (internal consistency) and fidelity (match with reality and values). sard can borrow this as two separate checks: does the narrative hang together, and does it match the source?

## Findings

**The narrative advantage is real but conditional.** The best quantitative evidence, Mar et al.'s meta-analysis [2.3], finds stories are understood and remembered better than expository text, with a larger effect on memory than on comprehension. Willingham explains why: causal links form "a web of associations", so recalling a goal lets the reader rebuild what followed [2.2]. Causal-network research makes the same point at the level of single events, which are remembered in proportion to their place on the causal chain [2.16]. Dahlstrom extends the finding to science communication for non-experts [2.5]. This is solid ground for sard's core claim. The caveats are just as clear, though. Mar et al. say the advantage comes from familiar structure, explicitly warn against forcing everything into narrative form, and report publication bias. Willingham treats story as a support for engaging with the subject, not a substitute.

**Understanding means grasping dependence, and that is what sard's connectors encode.** Philosophy of science and epistemology define understanding as grasping explanatory and dependence relations, not holding isolated propositions [2.6]. The interventionist account adds a practical test: a good explanation tells you what would change if a factor were different, and it leaves out factors that make no difference [2.8]. Kintsch's model places this in the reader's mind. The goal is a situation model built from the text plus prior knowledge [2.15], which is what sard's "from the fact to its meaning" rule targets. sard does not yet check whether that model was actually built. The illusion of explanatory depth shows that felt understanding of causal content is easily inflated [2.33].

**Explicit connectives help, but how much they help depends on the reader.** Causal markers improve comprehension of expository text, including for second-language readers [2.12], and for readers without domain knowledge they can be what makes comprehension possible [2.13]. The reverse cohesion effect, however, shows that high-knowledge readers learn more deeply from less explicit text that forces them to infer the links [2.14]. sard's rule of a bold connector in every paragraph is ideal for novices and readers working across languages, but it may over-scaffold experts. Coherence-relation theory [2.11] and RST [2.10] provide a richer and more honest relation inventory than sard's current list: additive vs causal, positive vs negative (concession/contrast), evidence, elaboration, condition and background. Not every pair of stations should be joined by "therefore".

**Front-loading meaning competes with delaying it, and the evidence supports a hybrid.** The inverted pyramid [2.24] and Minto's pyramid [2.23] put the conclusion first because readers stop early and decision-makers want the answer. Signaling research shows that cued content is remembered and uncued content is not, and that headings outperform preview sentences for topic structure [2.17]. Advance organizers help, most of all for weaker learners [2.18]. sard already has a "compass" and a "foundation", which act as an advance organizer, but it leaves the compass optional and holds the message until the end. Minto's SCQ opening is itself a short narrative of situation, complication and question, so an answer-first frame and a narrative body can coexist.

**Narrative needs tension, and sard lacks a "But".** Willingham's conflict [2.2], Olson's ABT [2.25], Minto's complication [2.23] and Made to Stick's "unexpected" [2.22] all say the same thing: an explanation moves because a problem or gap pulls it forward. A sequence of "and then" is a list in disguise. sard's connector list includes "however", but its structure never requires naming the question or knot the idea resolves. sard-roots has implicitly found this: "the problem the idea was born from" is the conflict. Mensink et al. add a nuance [2.4]. Introductions improve recall, but narrative introductions slow processing, so the tension should come from the content's own problem and not from a decorative anecdote.

**Narrative distorts in predictable ways, and each one maps to a sard failure mode.** The narrative fallacy is forcing an arrow of causation onto a sequence [2.30]. In sard this means writing "that's why" where the source only says "and". The conjunction fallacy shows that added specifics raise plausibility while lowering probability [2.31], which is what happens when an explainer adds vivid context the source lacks. Lombrozo shows that people over-prefer single-cause stories [2.32], so a tidy chain can quietly erase the author's multiple causes. Danto's narrative sentences [2.9] and the philosophy of history [2.7] show that retrospective narration makes outcomes look inevitable, and that several truthful narratives of the same events can exist. Transportation research shows that absorbed readers counterargue less [2.34]. Smooth narration of an argument therefore persuades, which matters for sard-article. Fisher's split between coherence and fidelity [2.35] names the core risk: a narrative can be internally coherent and still unfaithful.

**Irrelevant vividness hurts learning.** Seductive details reduce both recall and transfer [2.19], and dead-end events are forgotten and judged unimportant [2.16]. sard-roots and sard-book pull in external history and anecdotes. These help when they sit on the causal path to the arrival, and they hurt when they are merely interesting.

**Expert writers' blind spot is the main source of bad explanation.** The curse of knowledge [2.20][2.21] predicts that the explainer, here Claude after reading the whole source, will skip definitions and leave logical steps unstated. Pinker's remedies are concrete instances, classic style that shows the reader the thing, and testing on a representative reader [2.20]. The Heaths add concreteness [2.22]. Distill adds that real distillation sometimes means reframing the idea with a better analogy or abstraction [2.26]. Graham adds that the struggle to put an idea into words reveals gaps [2.28], and that qualifications are how a writer states their degree of certainty [2.27].

**Narrative and reference are different products.** Bruner's two modes [2.1] and Vox's split between linear explainers and modular cards [2.29] suggest sard should recognize when the user needs a path (narrative) and when they need a lookup (cards, tables). sard-deck's study deck is the natural home for the second, and the core sard should not try to be both.

## Gaps in sard today
- No audience calibration. Connector density, foundation depth and term definitions are the same for a novice and an expert, despite the reverse cohesion effect [2.14] and the curse of knowledge [2.20].
- No tension slot. The structure never names the problem or question the idea answers. "However" exists as a connector but is not required [2.2][2.25].
- Connector inventory is shallow and leans causal. There is no additive, evidence, concession or condition category grounded in coherence-relation theory, which nudges toward "that's why" even when the source only juxtaposes [2.10][2.11][2.30].
- The compass is optional and the meaning comes last. There is no answer-first mode for decision or reference requests [2.23][2.24].
- No material-shape detection. Taxonomies, procedures, data comparisons and proofs are forced into the same station chain, against Mar et al.'s explicit warning [2.3][2.1].
- No guards against narrative distortion: invented vivid detail, collapsing several causes into one, hindsight inevitability, or a single story where several exist [2.31][2.32][2.9][2.7].
- No relevance filter for anecdotes and background, which matters especially in sard-roots and sard-book [2.19][2.16].
- No certainty marking beyond separating "a note of my own" [2.27].
- No check that the reader actually understood. The "next step" line offers more content, never a self-test [2.33][2.8].
- No "gap" protocol. When the explainer cannot connect two stations honestly, nothing says to report it instead of smoothing over it [2.28].
- sard-article narrates an argument without guarding against narrative persuasion (smooth flow read as validity) [2.34][2.35].

## Proposals for sard
- P2.1: **Typed connectors.** Replace the connector list with a small relation table based on Sanders' primitives and RST: causal (because/that's why), additive (alongside this/also), contrast and concession (but/yet/despite), temporal (then), evidence/example (for instance/the proof is), condition (only if), elaboration (more precisely). Add a rule: when the source states no causal link, use an additive or temporal connector and never upgrade a sequence into a cause. (sources: 2.10, 2.11, 2.30) — Why it fits: it makes "connectors must be logically true" checkable without abandoning bold connectors.
- P2.2: **The knot (العقدة).** Make one sentence mandatory, in the foundation or compass, that states the problem, gap or question the idea answers, and require at least one honest "But" station. sard-roots already does this at length, and the core should do it in one line. (sources: 2.2, 2.25, 2.23, 2.22) — Why it fits: tension is what turns stations from a list into a narrative.
- P2.3: **Reader dial.** Before writing, infer the reader's level from the request, or ask once when it is unclear and the output is long. Novice or second-language reader: a full foundation, every term defined, a connector on every station. Expert: a short foundation, connectors only where the relation is not obvious, and more room for critique and the explainer's own note. (sources: 2.12, 2.13, 2.14, 2.18, 2.21) — Why it fits: the same narrative spine, scaffolded to the reader.
- P2.4: **Compass rules.** The compass becomes mandatory above about 400 words. For decision or reference requests ("what's the conclusion?", reports, briefs), use answer-first mode: put "the message in brief" at the top as the compass, then narrate how we get there. For long outputs, allow short headings over station groups. (sources: 2.23, 2.24, 2.17) — Why it fits: the reader knows the destination and the path is still narrated.
- P2.5: **Shape check before stations.** Classify the material as causal process or history, argument, taxonomy or parallel set, procedure, or data/comparison. Narrate fully only causal processes and arguments. For the other shapes, narrate the organizing principle and why it matters, then allow a compact table or numbered steps, each step carrying its "why". (sources: 2.3, 2.1, 2.29) — Why it fits: narrative governs the meaning layer without distorting non-narrative content.
- P2.6: **Fidelity checks** added to the pre-send list: (a) every causal connector can be traced to the source or is marked as inference; (b) if the source gives several causes, all are kept; (c) no vivid specifics appear that are not in the source; (d) known outcomes are not narrated as inevitable; (e) on contested topics, add one line: "another way to tell this…". (sources: 2.30, 2.31, 2.32, 2.9, 2.7, 2.35) — Why it fits: it protects the honesty the narrative depends on.
- P2.7: **On-the-path rule for detail.** Every anecdote, historical fact or example must feed the next station or the arrival. Cut it, or move it to a clearly marked aside. This applies especially to sard-roots and sard-book external research. (sources: 2.19, 2.16) — Why it fits: dead ends weaken the chain the reader remembers.
- P2.8: **One concrete anchor per abstract station.** Use a case, number, quotation or analogy, preferably from the source. When using an analogy, state where it breaks. (sources: 2.20, 2.22, 2.26) — Why it fits: it moves the reader from fact to meaning through something they can see.
- P2.9: **Understanding check as an option for the "next step".** Alongside "next chapter?", offer one what-if or why question the reader can answer only if they built the model, for example "what would change if X were absent?". Feed these questions to sard-deck's study deck. (sources: 2.8, 2.33, 2.15, 2.6) — Why it fits: it tests the meaning the narrative claimed to deliver, at the cost of one line.
- P2.10: **Gap protocol.** If no honest connector can be written between two stations, re-read the source. If the gap is real, say so in the narrative ("the author does not explain how X leads to Y") and do not paper over it. (sources: 2.28, 2.26) — Why it fits: an honest gap is part of an honest story.
- P2.11: **Calibrated hedges.** Separate "the author states", "the evidence suggests" and "it is unclear whether" within stations, not only through "a note of my own". (sources: 2.27, 2.5) — Why it fits: the explainer's confidence becomes part of the reader's map.
- P2.12: **Persuasion guard in sard-article.** After narrating how an argument flows, add a separate check of whether it holds (coherence vs fidelity), so that narrative smoothness is not read as validity. (sources: 2.34, 2.35, 2.5) — Why it fits: the reader keeps the narrative's clarity without its persuasive bias.
- P2.13: **Nucleus-first stations.** The bold connector is followed immediately by the station's core claim, and support comes afterwards. The existing "read only the first words" test becomes "read only the first sentence of each paragraph". (sources: 2.10, 2.17) — Why it fits: the skeleton of first sentences is itself a complete mini-narrative.

## Cautions
- **Template creep dilutes the core.** ABT, SCQA, the pyramid, SUCCESs and the 4 Cs should become internal moves (the knot, the compass, the concrete anchor) and not user-facing modes or labels. If sard starts emitting "Situation:/Complication:" headers, it is a consulting template and no longer narration.
- **Over-narration.** Mar et al. [2.3] warn explicitly against forcing information into narrative form. A taxonomy told as a story is less accurate and harder to look up. The shape check (P2.5) must be allowed to say "this part is a table".
- **Over-connecting.** Explicit connectors on every line can patronize experts and suppress the inference that produces deep learning [2.14]. Repetitive connectors (a run of «ثم يتضح» in Arabic) read as AI filler. Connectors carry logic and should not appear by quota.
- **Narrative makes claims feel truer than the evidence supports.** Narrative reduces counterarguing [2.34], invented detail raises false plausibility [2.31], single-cause chains are over-preferred [2.32], and hindsight makes outcomes look inevitable [2.9]. The better sard tells a story, the more it can mislead, so fidelity checks matter more as fluency improves.
- **Hooks are not free.** Narrative introductions slow processing [2.4] and seductive details hurt transfer [2.19]. Tension should come from the material's own problem and not from a borrowed anecdote.
- **Do not pretend there is only one story.** History and contested fields admit several truthful narratives [2.7]. A single confident arc can erase a real debate.
- **Bloat risk.** Each proposal above adds checklist items. Keep SKILL.md short: fold P2.6, P2.10 and P2.13 into the existing pre-send check, and keep the reader dial (P2.3) and shape check (P2.5) to a few lines each. A skill too long for the model to follow is worse than one missing a rule.
- **Evidence caveat.** Most narrative-advantage studies use school or lab texts and short-term recall, and the meta-analysis shows publication bias [2.3]. Treat the advantage as robust in direction but probably overstated in size.
