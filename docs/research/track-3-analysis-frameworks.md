# Track 3: Reading, analysis and critical thinking
## Sources
- [3.1] How to Read a Book (Adler & Van Doren) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/How_to_Read_a_Book — encyclopedia — Adler's four reader questions ("What is it about as a whole? What is said in detail, and how? Is it true? What of it?") line up with sard's compass/foundation → stations → (missing) judgment → arrival, so sard already covers three of them and lacks only "Is it true?".
- [3.2] How to Read a Book — Farnam Street — https://fs.blog/how-to-read-a-book/ — blog — Analytical reading means classifying the work, stating its unity in brief, outlining its parts *in their order and relation*, and naming the problem the author is solving. That is sard's preparation step. Syntopical reading adds four moves sard lacks for multiple sources: find the relevant passages, translate each author's terms into one neutral vocabulary, frame questions every author answers, and define the issues where they disagree.
- [3.3] S. Keshav, How to Read a Paper — https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/fall10/cos597B/papers/howtoread-keshav.pdf — paper — The three-pass method (5–10 min overview, about 1 h for content, then a deep "virtual re-implementation") and the first-pass "five Cs" (Category, Context, Correctness, Contributions, Clarity) give a ready-made inspection step and a depth dial for research papers.
- [3.4] Greenhalgh, How to Read a Paper (BMJ series) — https://thebmj-frontend.bmj.com/about-bmj/resources-readers/publications/how-read-paper — docs — "Getting your bearings" (what is the study about, what design, was the design right for the question) is the paper equivalent of sard's foundation. You cannot narrate a study's findings honestly until you have narrated its design.
- [3.5] Sutherland, Spiegelhalter & Burgman, Twenty tips for interpreting scientific claims (Nature 2013) — https://www.cambridgenetwork.co.uk/news/twenty-top-tips-interpreting-scientific-claims — news — Twenty compact checks (chance causes variation, no measurement is exact, correlation is not causation, cherry-picking, regression to the mean, extrapolation). Together they act as a guardrail that stops a smooth narrative from overstating a statistical finding.
- [3.6] SQ3R method (Portland CC) — https://www.pcc.edu/panther-tracks/study-skills/sq3r/ — docs — "Turn each heading into a question" is the student-side mirror of sard's rule that every station answers an implicit "why do we need this now?". In study mode, sard could show that question.
- [3.7] PQ4R method (University of York) — https://subjectguides.york.ac.uk/study-revision/pq4r-method — docs — PQ4R adds a *Reflect* step (how does this connect to what I already know?). That is a legitimate home for the "note of my own" and for linking to the reader's prior knowledge.
- [3.8] How to perform a close reading (UW Odegaard Writing Center) — https://depts.washington.edu/owrc/undergraduate-students/handouts/how-to-perform-close-reading — docs — Close reading works on a small passage, notices word choice, image and syntax, and then must tie each detail back to a central claim. This is "from fact to meaning" at the level of the sentence, a mode sard lacks for poetry, scripture and dense philosophy.
- [3.9] Hermeneutics (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy) — https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hermeneutics/ — encyclopedia — The hermeneutic circle (the parts are understood through the whole and the whole through the parts) and Gadamer's point that the interpreter's pre-understanding always shapes reading both justify sard's "foundation first" and its separation of the author's words from the explainer's view.
- [3.10] Hermeneutic Circle (Simply Psychology) — https://www.simplypsychology.org/hermeneutic-circle.html — blog — The "fusion of horizons" means the interpreter's horizon is never erased, only made explicit. In sard, the "note of my own" is that horizon declared openly, so it should also carry the explainer's assumptions.
- [3.11] Toulmin's Argument Model (Lumen / SUNY) — https://courses.lumenlearning.com/suny-jeffersoncc-englishcomp2kscopexmaster/chapter/toulmins-argument-model/ — docs — Claim, grounds, warrant, backing, qualifier and rebuttal map almost one-to-one onto honest narrative connectors ("because", "and this only works if", "which rests on", "at least usually", "unless"). The often-unstated warrant is exactly what a narrative reconstruction should surface.
- [3.12] Argument map (Wikipedia) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argument_map — encyclopedia — The distinction between co-premises (dependent) and independent premises, and the purpose of exposing unstated assumptions, give sard a vocabulary for "this station needs the previous one" versus "this is a separate, parallel support".
- [3.13] Argument Maps Improve Critical Thinking (Twardy) — https://web-archive.southampton.ac.uk/cogprints.org/3008/ — paper — Reports that computer-assisted argument mapping roughly tripled critical-thinking gains compared with other methods. This is evidence that making argument structure explicit, not only reading prose, is what teaches analysis.
- [3.14] Using Computer-Aided Argument Mapping to Teach Reasoning (eCampusOntario) — https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/criticalthinking1234/chapter/introduction/ — book — Cites van Gelder (2015): high-intensity mapping courses gain about 0.8 SD, more than twice typical CT courses. This supports an *optional* compact argument skeleton next to the narrative, not instead of it.
- [3.15] Walton, Reed & Macagno, Argumentation Schemes (Cambridge UP) — https://assets.cambridge.org/97805218/86178/frontmatter/9780521886178_frontmatter.pdf — book — Each common argument type (expert opinion, cause to effect, analogy, consequences) comes with its own *critical questions*. These are a better critique engine for sard than fallacy labels, because they ask what would have to hold, not what to call the error.
- [3.16] Fallacies (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy) — https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/fallacies/ — encyclopedia — Textbook fallacy lists rarely match real discourse, and dialectical theories (pragma-dialectics, Walton) treat a fallacy as misuse in context. Sard should avoid fallacy-label hunting and explain *why* a move fails in this particular exchange.
- [3.17] Arguments vs. Explanations (LibreTexts, Critical Thinking) — https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Courses/Cosumnes_River_College/SOC_305%3A_Critical_Thinking_in_Social_Science_(Lugo)/04%3A_What_is_an_Argument/4.05%3A_Arguments_vs._Explanations — docs — An explanation sheds light on something accepted as fact, while an argument tries to establish it. Sard's form is explanatory, so when the source is an *argument* sard must not narrate its contested conclusion as if it were established ("this is why" vs "the author argues that, because").
- [3.18] Principle of charity (Wikipedia) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principle_of_charity — encyclopedia — Interpret a text in its most rational, strongest form whenever a coherent reading is available. Sard should make this an explicit rule for ambiguous passages and before any critique.
- [3.19] Of Strawmen and Steelwomen (Conceptually) — https://conceptually.org/concepts/of-strawmen-and-steelwomen — blog — Steelmanning rebuilds the strongest version of a view, sometimes stronger than the author stated it. This is a useful but risky move for sard: any strengthening must be labeled as the explainer's, never the author's.
- [3.20] Rapoport's Rules (Dennett) — https://www.rightattitudes.com/2017/06/16/rapoport-rules-criticism/ — blog — Before criticizing, restate the position so well its author would thank you, then list agreements, then what you learned, and only then rebut. This gives sard's optional critique a ready, non-hostile order that a narrative can carry naturally.
- [3.21] Ideological Turing Tests (LessWrong wiki) — https://lesswrong.com/w/ideological-turing-tests — blog — State an opposing view so that its holders cannot tell it from their own. This is a concrete quality bar for a "compare two positions" mode.
- [3.22] Rogerian Argument (Writing Commons) — https://writingcommons.org/section/genre/argument-argumentation/rogerian-argument/ — docs — Opening from common ground and a fair statement of the other side gives a narrative order for explaining a disagreement without taking sides.
- [3.23] Stasis Theory (Purdue OWL) — https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/the_writing_process/stasis_theory/stasis_for_teamwork.html — docs — The four stasis questions (fact, definition, quality, policy) locate *where* two parties actually disagree. That is the ideal foundation paragraph for mapping a debate: "they agree on the facts but split on definition".
- [3.24] Rhetorical Analysis (Kennesaw State Writing Center) — https://writingcenter.kennesaw.edu/resources/handouts/rhetorical_analysis.php — docs — The rhetorical situation (speaker, audience, purpose) and the appeals (ethos, pathos, logos) offer a "how does this persuade?" lens for speeches, ads, op-eds and sermons, a question sard currently never asks.
- [3.25] Socratic questioning (Univ. of Michigan, Fogler & Gurmen) — https://public.websites.umich.edu/~elements/fogler%26gurmen/html/probsolv/strategy/cthinking.htm — docs — Paul's six question types (clarification, assumptions, evidence, viewpoints, implications, questions about the question) can serve as a pre-writing probe and as end-of-narrative check questions for a study deck.
- [3.26] Paul–Elder Critical Thinking Framework (Univ. of Louisville) — https://student.louisville.edu/paul-elder-critical-thinking-framework — docs — The eight elements of reasoning (purpose, question, information, inference, concepts, assumptions, implications, point of view) are a complete hidden checklist for finding sard's stations. The nine standards (clarity, accuracy, precision, relevance, depth, breadth, logic, significance, fairness) can serve as its "check before sending".
- [3.27] Critical Thinking (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Hitchcock) — https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/critical-thinking/ — encyclopedia — Defines critical thinking as careful, goal-directed thinking and lists dispositions such as suspending judgment, seeking alternatives and self-correction. The skill should model these, for example by saying "the text does not settle this" when it doesn't.
- [3.28] Revised Bloom's Taxonomy (Iowa State CELT) — https://www.celt.iastate.edu/teaching/effective-practice/revised-blooms-taxonomy/ — docs — Understand/explain, analyze (break into parts and their relations), evaluate (judge against criteria) and create are distinct cognitive acts. Sard currently delivers *understand*, so the redesign needs a router for the others.
- [3.29] Summary vs. Analysis (Reynolds CC Writing Studio) — https://reynolds.edu/writing-studio/writing-resources/summary-vs-analysis.html — docs — A summary asks who/what/where and stays neutral, while an analysis asks how/why and carries the writer's inference. Sard sits between them (explanation), and saying this openly helps it tell users what they will get.
- [3.30] Comparing and Contrasting (UNC Writing Center) — https://writingcenter.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/346/2012/09/Comparing-and-Contrasting-The-Writing-Center.pdf — docs — A comparison needs a frame of reference, grounds for comparison, a thesis and a "so what?". It can be organized text-by-text or point-by-point, and the "so what?" is exactly sard's *arrival*.
- [3.31] How to synthesise information (University of Sheffield) — https://sheffield.ac.uk/study-skills/research/approaches/synthesise — docs — Group sources by theme, not source by source, and make relations explicit with linking words ("similarly", "in contrast", "builds on"). In effect this is sard's honest-connector rule applied across sources.
- [3.32] How I Made My Life Easier By Using A Synthesis Matrix (UNC Writing Center) — https://writingcenter.unc.edu/2022/03/synthesis-matrix — docs — A source × theme grid is the right *internal* scaffold for multi-source narration. The user sees the narrative by theme, not the grid.
- [3.33] First Principles (Farnam Street) — https://fs.blog/first-principles/ — blog — Separates what is known to be true from assumption and holds that going "a level or two deeper" is usually enough. This is a useful stopping rule that aligns with sard-roots' three-layer limit.
- [3.34] Munger's Worldly Wisdom (Farnam Street) — https://fs.blog/2015/09/munger-worldly-wisdom/ — blog — The "latticework" idea of borrowing models from other fields fits the "note of my own": an explicitly labeled cross-field lens, kept apart from the author's content.
- [3.35] Five whys (Wikipedia) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_whys — encyclopedia — Ohno's chain of "why" is naturally narrative, but critics, including people at Toyota, note it stops at symptoms, is limited by what the investigator already knows, is not repeatable and forces a single root cause.
- [3.36] Card, The problem with "5 whys" (BMJ Quality & Safety 2017) — https://qualitysafety.bmj.com/content/26/8/671 — paper — Single causal chains oversimplify multi-causal failures. A causal narrative must allow branches ("and in parallel…") and back each link with evidence, not off-the-cuff deduction.
- [3.37] Ishikawa diagram (Wikipedia) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ishikawa_diagram — encyclopedia — The cause categories (6Ms, or People/Process/Technology/Policy for services) work as a hidden coverage check, so a "why did this happen" narrative does not miss a whole family of causes.
- [3.38] Hill's nine criteria for causal association (Hapgood) — https://hapgood.us/2012/02/03/hills-nine-criteria-for-causal-association/ — blog — Strength, consistency, temporality, dose–response and plausibility give sard a principled test before it writes a causal connector ("because", "led to") about a real-world claim.
- [3.39] Analysis of competing hypotheses (Wikipedia) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analysis_of_competing_hypotheses — encyclopedia — List rival explanations and test the evidence *against* each, aiming to reject rather than confirm. This is a discipline for "why" and "what's going on" questions so the narrative does not lock onto the first story.
- [3.40] Heuer, Psychology of Intelligence Analysis (CIA) — https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/Pyschology-of-Intelligence-Analysis.pdf — book — Mental models and cognitive biases shape analysis. A coherent story is itself a bias risk, which supports keeping an explicit "what would change this conclusion" line.
- [3.41] Abduction (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy) — https://plato.stanford.edu/ENTRIES/abduction/ — encyclopedia — Inference to the best explanation is how the "note of my own" usually reasons. Naming it lets sard say "the best explanation I can see is X, though Y also fits".
- [3.42] Lombrozo, Explanatory Preferences and the Role of Explanation in Learning (2016) — https://cognition.princeton.edu/sites/default/files/cognition/files/explanatory_prefs_tics.pdf — paper — People prefer simple, broad explanations, and generating explanations aids learning, but the same preference pushes people toward overly simple accounts. This is the main scientific caution for a narrative-first skill.
- [3.43] What is GRADE? (BMJ Best Practice) — https://bestpractice.bmj.com/info/evidence/learn-ebm/what-is-grade/ — docs — Four certainty levels (high, moderate, low, very low), with downgrades for bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias. This offers a plain-language qualifier vocabulary sard can attach to claims.
- [3.44] CRAAP Test (Merritt College) — https://merritt.libguides.com/CRAAP_Test — docs — Currency, relevance, authority, accuracy and purpose are a familiar checklist. Its *purpose* question (inform, sell, persuade) is the useful part for sard's context step, but as a checklist it is weaker than lateral reading [3.46].
- [3.45] SIFT: The Four Moves (Caulfield) — https://hapgood.us/2019/06/19/sift-the-four-moves/ — blog — Stop, investigate the source, find better coverage, and trace claims to the original. This is a lightweight protocol sard-roots, sard-article and sard-book can run before trusting web material they cite.
- [3.46] Wineburg & McGrew, Lateral Reading and the Nature of Expertise — https://cor.inquirygroup.org/research/lateral-reading-and-the-nature-of-expertise — paper — Fact-checkers beat historians and students by leaving the page to check what others say about a source ("reading less and learning more"). Sard should verify context laterally rather than trust a source's self-presentation.
- [3.47] How Charts Lie (Cairo, via FlowingData) — https://flowingdata.com/2019/10/25/how-charts-lie — blog — Charts mislead through scale, wrong or incomplete data and hidden uncertainty. For slides and articles with figures, sard should first narrate what a chart encodes, then what it shows, then what it cannot show.
- [3.48] Pre-mortem (Wikipedia) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-mortem — encyclopedia — Imagine the decision has already failed and explain why. This narrative-native technique for analyzing a plan or decision fits sard's story form directly.

## Findings
Across the reading literature, sard's core already matches the most-cited model of deep reading. Adler's analytical rules (state the unity, outline the parts *in their order and relation*, name the author's problem) are essentially sard's preparation step, and his first, second and fourth questions ("what is it about", "what is said and how", "what of it") match foundation, stations and arrival [3.1][3.2]. The hermeneutic circle gives a philosophical reason why "foundation before stations" works: parts are only intelligible against a grasp of the whole [3.9]. The one Adler question sard does not ask systematically is "Is it true?". Its only answer today is the optional critical pause in sard-article.

The frameworks consistently separate four cognitive acts that sard currently merges: summarizing (who/what, neutral), explaining (why/how something accepted is so), analyzing (breaking into parts and relations) and evaluating (judging against criteria) [3.28][3.29][3.17]. Sard is an *explanation* engine. That is its strength, but when the input is an argument, an explanatory form can quietly turn the author's contested conclusion into narrated fact. Critical-thinking texts treat "argument vs explanation" as near-orthodox, and this is the most important conceptual boundary for the redesign [3.17].

Argument analysis has the strongest evidence base of anything in this track. Making argument structure explicit through argument mapping produces critical-thinking gains of about 0.8 SD, roughly double ordinary courses [3.13][3.14]. Toulmin's six parts map naturally onto connectors, and the hidden warrant is precisely the "foundation" a reader is missing [3.11]. Walton-style argument schemes with critical questions give a principled way to probe each kind of support [3.15]. Meanwhile, the SEP warns that fallacy catalogues rarely fit real discourse, so labeling is a weak analysis tool [3.16]. The lesson for sard is to reconstruct before judging and to judge through questions rather than labels.

Fairness frameworks converge on one order of operations: understand fully and charitably first, then criticize. That order appears in Adler's "I understand" before "I agree/disagree", the principle of charity, steelmanning, Rapoport's rules and the ideological Turing test [3.2][3.18][3.19][3.20][3.21]. Rapoport's sequence (restate, list agreements, say what you learned, then rebut) is already narrative in shape. Steelmanning is useful but dangerous for sard, because a strengthened version must never be attributed to the author. That is an extension of sard's existing author vs explainer separation.

Multi-source understanding (syntopical reading, literature-review synthesis, compare-and-contrast) is a clear gap. Its methods share a structure: a frame of reference and neutral shared terms, questions every source answers, issues where they diverge, and a "so what?" [3.2][3.30][3.31][3.32]. Stasis theory adds the sharpest tool for debates. It locates the disagreement at the level of fact, definition, quality or policy, which makes an excellent foundation paragraph [3.23]. Synthesis guides even recommend explicit linking words ("similarly", "in contrast"), which is sard's connector discipline applied across sources [3.31]. The internal scaffold (a matrix) should stay hidden, and the output stays narrative by theme.

Evidence evaluation is the second major gap. Sard's rule that connectors must be logically true covers the logic of the links but not the strength of the evidence behind them. GRADE offers a four-level certainty vocabulary with explicit downgrade reasons [3.43]. Keshav's five Cs and Greenhalgh's "getting your bearings" provide an inspection pass for papers [3.3][3.4], and Sutherland et al.'s twenty tips list the statistical traps a fluent narrative most easily hides [3.5]. For web sources, the evidence favors lateral reading and SIFT over static checklists like CRAAP [3.44][3.45][3.46]. This matters because sard-roots and sard-book already search the web.

Causal and "why did this happen" analysis is a natural narrative mode, but a risky one. The 5 Whys is literally a story of connectors, yet both Toyota insiders and a BMJ Quality & Safety critique show it oversimplifies multi-causal failures, stops at symptoms and is not repeatable [3.35][3.36]. Ishikawa categories as a coverage check, Hill's criteria before asserting causation, and analysis of competing hypotheses (seeking disconfirming evidence for rival stories) together turn a causal narrative from plausible into defensible [3.37][3.38][3.39][3.40].

Cognitive science supports both the narrative idea and its main risk. Generating explanations helps learning, but people's preference for simple, broad explanations also leads them to accept reductive accounts [3.42]. Heuer makes the same point for analysts: a coherent mental model resists disconfirming data [3.40]. A narrative that is "smooth" can therefore feel truer than it is. Honest qualifiers, explicit branches and a "what would change this" line are the counterweights.

Several frameworks fit sard as *hidden* scaffolds rather than visible output. Paul–Elder's elements of reasoning and Socratic question types are ideal for finding stations and testing the draft [3.25][3.26]. SQ3R/PQ4R suggest exposing each station's implicit question in study mode [3.6][3.7]. Close reading and rhetorical analysis add two lenses sard lacks: sentence-level reading for dense or literary passages, and "how does this persuade?" for persuasive texts [3.8][3.24]. Data-heavy material needs a chart-reading discipline (what is encoded, what it shows, what it hides) [3.47]. Pre-mortems and first-principles reasoning offer narrative-native ways to analyze decisions and ideas [3.48][3.33].

## Gaps in sard today
- No step that decides *what kind* of understanding the user wants (explain, analyze an argument, evaluate, compare, synthesize, find causes, weigh a decision). Everything becomes an explanation.
- No guard against narrating an *argument* as an *explanation*: "this is why" can quietly endorse a contested claim.
- Critique exists only as an optional pause in sard-article, with no method: no reconstruction of implicit warrants, no critical questions, no charity-first order.
- No mode for two or more texts or positions: comparison, a debate map or a syntopical/literature synthesis.
- Connector honesty covers logical relations but not *evidential strength*. There is no certainty vocabulary and no way to say "suggests" vs "shows".
- No source-credibility protocol for web material that sard-roots and sard-book already rely on (no lateral reading or SIFT).
- No guidance for research papers: study design, statistics, the five Cs or common traps.
- No causal or root-cause mode (sard-roots covers the history of ideas, not the causes of events or problems), and no protection against single-chain causation.
- No decision or options analysis mode.
- No chart or data reading discipline beyond inventorying slides.
- No handling of interpretive ambiguity: when a passage supports two readings, sard picks one silently.
- No close-reading mode for a short dense passage, and no rhetorical "how it persuades" lens.
- No depth levels beyond a word-count rule (inspectional vs analytical vs deep).

## Proposals for sard
- P3.1: **Add an intent router (the destination type) to the core.** Before preparing, sard decides which act is requested: *explain* (default), *analyze an argument*, *evaluate*, *compare/synthesize*, *find causes*, or *weigh a decision*. It then picks the matching arrival sentence ("the meaning is…", "the argument stands or falls on…", "they really disagree about…", "the most likely cause is…", "the choice turns on…"). Structure, connectors and prose rules stay identical. (sources: 3.28, 3.29, 3.17, 3.1) — Only the *destination* changes, so every mode remains a journey from a foundation to an arrival.
- P3.2: **New core rule: "argument ≠ explanation".** When the source argues for a claim, narrate it as the author's reasoning ("the author argues that…, because…, which only works if…") and never with the explainer's own "that's why". Use stronger connectors only for what is established. (sources: 3.17, 3.42) — This extends the existing "connectors must be logically true" rule to "connectors must not endorse what the source only argues".
- P3.3: **Turn sard-article's critical pause into a charity-first evaluation sequence, usable by any command.** The order: (1) restate the position so its author would accept it, (2) surface the implicit warrant and the qualifier the claim needs, (3) points of agreement, (4) the scheme's critical questions (expert, cause, analogy, consequences), (5) where it is weakest, and (6) what evidence would change the verdict. Prefer critical questions to fallacy labels. (sources: 3.11, 3.15, 3.16, 3.18, 3.20, 3.2) — Rapoport's order is already a narrative arc, and it keeps the author vs explainer separation intact.
- P3.4: **New command `/sard-compare` covering two positions, texts or options, and N sources in syntopical mode.** The foundation states the frame of reference, the neutral shared terms, and *where* the disagreement sits (stasis level: fact, definition, quality or policy). The stations go point by point with honest cross-source connectors ("similarly", "in contrast", "where they part ways", "this builds on"). Each side must pass an ideological-Turing check. The arrival is the "so what?". A synthesis matrix may be used internally but is never shown as the answer. (sources: 3.2, 3.21, 3.23, 3.30, 3.31, 3.32, 3.22) — Comparison becomes a story of two paths that converge and split, still prose and still connector-driven.
- P3.5: **Add certainty-aware qualifiers ("honest strength") to the core rules.** Every load-bearing factual or causal claim carries a plain qualifier drawn from a GRADE-like scale (established, likely, uncertain, contested), and causal connectors require at least temporality plus some consistency or plausibility (Hill). For papers, add a sard-article branch with Keshav's five Cs, Greenhalgh's "what design, and was it right", and the Sutherland traps. (sources: 3.43, 3.38, 3.3, 3.4, 3.5) — Qualifiers live inside the sentences of the narrative, so the story stays fluent but stops overstating.
- P3.6: **Add a lateral-reading protocol (SIFT) wherever sard searches the web** (sard-roots, sard-book, sard-article context): investigate the source, find better coverage, trace each claim to its origin, and only then use it in the foundation. (sources: 3.45, 3.46, 3.44) — This protects the foundation paragraph, which everything else is built on.
- P3.7: **Add a causal mode ("why did this happen?"), as a branch of the router or folded into sard-roots.** The causal chain is the stations, but it allows parallel branches ("and alongside this…"), runs Ishikawa categories as a hidden coverage check, and names at least one rival explanation with the evidence that weakens it (ACH). The arrival states the best explanation *and* its confidence. (sources: 3.35, 3.36, 3.37, 3.39, 3.40, 3.41) — Causes are already a story, and this keeps the story honest about branching and alternatives.
- P3.8: **Use Paul–Elder and Socratic questions as a hidden station finder and pre-send check.** Scan for purpose, question, concepts, assumptions, evidence, inferences, implications and point of view to find the stations. Add "fairness" and "significance" to the existing pre-send checklist. In study decks (sard-deck), end with 3–5 Socratic check questions. (sources: 3.25, 3.26, 3.27, 3.6, 3.7) — This improves the stations without changing the visible form.
- P3.9: **Add interpretive-ambiguity handling and a close-reading lens.** When a passage has two plausible readings, narrate the strongest (charity) and name the alternative in one clause. Offer a close-reading mode for a short dense passage (word, image, syntax, then the whole), plus a rhetorical lens for persuasive texts (audience, purpose, appeals). (sources: 3.8, 3.9, 3.10, 3.18, 3.24) — Close reading is "from fact to meaning" at the scale of the sentence.
- P3.10: **Add a chart and data rule for sard-slides and sard-article.** For every chart or table, narrate in order what it encodes (axes, scale, source), what it shows, and what it cannot show (uncertainty, missing comparison), before drawing meaning from it. (sources: 3.47, 3.5) — This is the fact-to-meaning rule applied to figures.
- P3.11: **Add a decision mode.** The foundation is the decision and its criteria. The stations cover the options and the evidence for each, with a pre-mortem station ("imagine this failed; the likeliest reason is…"). The arrival is what the choice turns on, not a recommendation unless asked. (sources: 3.48, 3.39, 3.33) — The pre-mortem is itself a narrative device.
- P3.12: **Add depth levels mapped to reading passes:** *quick* (compass plus the message in brief, similar to Keshav pass 1 or Adler's inspectional reading), *standard* (the current structure), and *deep* (plus the evaluation sequence and sources). (sources: 3.1, 3.3) — The same narrative at three zoom levels instead of different formats.
- P3.13: **Strengthen "a note of my own" as a declared horizon.** It states the lens used (a cross-field mental model or the best-explanation inference) and the assumption it depends on. (sources: 3.10, 3.34, 3.41) — This makes the existing separation more rigorous without adding sections.

## Cautions
- **Frameworks must run underneath, not on the page.** Toulmin tables, CRAAP checklists, fishbones, Paul–Elder grids and synthesis matrices are scaffolds. If they appear as the answer, sard turns back into bullet points. At most, an optional compact argument skeleton or a sources list goes after the narrative, as sard-article already allows.
- **Command sprawl dilutes the identity.** Prefer one router with modes (P3.1) and add at most one new command (`/sard-compare`). Causal, decision and close-reading work can be modes rather than separate commands.
- **Narrative coherence can manufacture certainty.** Lombrozo and Heuer show that simple, smooth stories feel truer than they are [3.42][3.40]. Connectors can quietly imply causation (post hoc). Qualifiers, branches and a "what would change this" line are non-negotiable if evaluation modes are added.
- **Evaluation must stay opt-in and proportional.** Users who ask "explain this" should not get a critique by default. Critique always comes after a charitable restatement and stays attributed to the explainer or named critics.
- **Do not hunt for fallacies.** Labels misfire in real discourse [3.16]. Explain why a step fails here, through critical questions.
- **Charity is not false balance.** Fair statement of each side does not mean equal evidential weight. Comparison must still report certainty differences [3.43].
- **Steelmanning can put words in the author's mouth.** Any strengthened version belongs in the explainer's voice, never in the author's.
- **The 5 Whys trap.** A single causal chain reads beautifully and is often wrong [3.36]. Causal narratives must allow parallel causes.
- **Checklist source evaluation is weaker than lateral reading** [3.46]. Do not adopt CRAAP verbatim.
- **Length creep.** Each added lens tempts extra paragraphs. Keep the core length rules, and let depth levels (P3.12) carry the extra material instead of every answer.
