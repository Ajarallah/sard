# Track 1: Learning science of explanation
## Sources
- [1.1] Why instructional explanations often do not work (Wittwer & Renkl 2008, Educational Psychologist 43), practitioner summary — https://theelearningcoach.com/elearning_design/instructional-explanations/ — blog — The central warning for sard: explanations usually fail because the reader stays passive and the explanation does not target their specific misunderstanding. Four fixes: make it adaptive, name the flaw before revising it, require active processing, and prompt self-explanation.
- [1.2] Learning from Instructional Explanations: Effects of Prompts Based on the Active-Constructive-Interactive Framework — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4390325/ — paper — A reduced explanation plus an inference prompt ("work out the missing step") beat a complete explanation plus a generic "write your thoughts" prompt. Remedial explanations combined with "what did you revise?" prompts did best. Leaving one deliberate gap and closing it after the reader tries is evidence-backed.
- [1.3] Using a diagnosis-based approach to individualize instructional explanations (Wittwer, Nückles & Renkl 2010) — https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10648-010-9118-7 — paper — In text-only settings, even a small diagnostic signal about the learner's understanding lets the explainer tailor the explanation, and tailored explanations deepen understanding. sard can ask one calibrating question or read the level from the request.
- [1.4] The ICAP Framework (Chi & Wylie 2014) — https://chilab.asu.edu/sites/default/files/lcl/chiwylie2014icap_2.pdf — paper — Engagement ranks Interactive > Constructive ≥ Active > Passive. Reading a sard narrative is passive or active at best, so a constructive step (explain back, answer a why-question) is what turns it into learning.
- [1.5] Bisra et al. 2018, Inducing self-explanation: a meta-analysis — https://summit.sfu.ca/item/34322 — paper — Self-explanation prompts give g ≈ .55 across domains, ages and task types, and the effect survives controls for time on task. This is the strongest single argument for ending sard with an "explain it back" invitation.
- [1.6] Dunlosky et al. 2013, Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques — https://www.readingrockets.org/resources/resource-library/improving-students-learning-effective-learning-techniques-promising — paper — Practice testing and distributed practice rate high utility. Self-explanation and elaborative interrogation (why-questions) rate moderate. Rereading and summarization rate low. A narrative that is only reread is in the low tier.
- [1.7] Cognitive Load Theory: Advances in Research on Worked Examples, Animations, and Cognitive Load Measurement — https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/cognitive-load-theory-advances-in-research-on-worked-examples-ani/ — paper — Working memory is overwhelmed by many interacting elements at once. Sequencing elements so each builds on the last (what sard's stations do) manages intrinsic load, and cutting what does not serve the idea reduces extraneous load.
- [1.8] Worked-example effect — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worked-example_effect — encyclopedia — For procedures (math, code, methods), studying a fully worked solution beats solving unaided for novices. A worked example narrated step by step with reasons is the procedural form of sard.
- [1.9] Renkl, Learning from worked-out examples: the transition from instructional explanations to self-explanation prompts — https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Alexander-Renkl/publication/252065774_Learning_from_worked-out_examples_The_transition_from_instructional_explanations_to_self-explanation_prompts/links/0c96053bbcc1ac1622000000/Learning-from-worked-out-examples-The-transition-from-instructional-explanations-to-self-explanation-prompts.pdf — paper — The recommended path starts with the explainer giving reasons and gradually shifts to prompting the learner to supply them. sard has no second phase.
- [1.10] Worked and faded examples (MIT Open Learning) — https://openlearning.mit.edu/mit-faculty/research-based-learning-findings/worked-and-faded-examples — docs — Fading means removing solution steps progressively so the learner completes more each time. This suits a procedural sard follow-up: one full narrated example, then one with the last step left to the reader.
- [1.11] Kalyuga 2007, Expertise reversal effect and its implications for learner-tailored instruction — https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/EDC608/Kalyuga2007_Article_ExpertiseReversalEffectAndItsI.pdf — paper — Support that helps novices (full context, every step explicit) can hurt experts through redundancy. One fixed sard depth cannot be right for everyone.
- [1.12] How less clarity could improve active processing (on McNamara et al. 1996, "Are good texts always better?") — https://editingresearch.byu.edu/2022/11/23/how-less-clarity-could-improve-active-processing — blog — This is the "reverse cohesion effect": low-knowledge readers gain from explicit coherence, while high-knowledge readers understand situations better from texts that leave inferences to them. It applies directly to how explicit sard's connectors should be.
- [1.13] Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006, Why minimal guidance during instruction does not work — https://www.davidlewisphd.com/courses/EDD8121/readings/2006-Kirschner_et_al.pdf — paper — Strong guidance beats discovery for novices, and its advantage fades only once learners have internal guidance (prior knowledge). This supports sard's guided, explicit stance for beginners.
- [1.14] Students who productively fail learn more (on Sinha & Kapur 2021 meta-analysis) — https://www.weforum.org/stories/2021/09/students-who-productively-fail-learn-more/ — news — Across 53 studies, trying a relevant problem before instruction improved conceptual learning, mainly for secondary and university students. This supports opening with the puzzle an idea answers. The finding is contested and design-dependent.
- [1.15] Subsumption Theory (Ausubel) — https://www.instructionaldesign.org/theories/subsumption-theory/ — blog — An advance organizer sits at a higher level of abstraction and inclusiveness than the material. It is not a summary or overview. sard's "compass" is currently a destination summary, while the "foundation" is closer to a true organizer.
- [1.16] Thirty years after Ausubel: an updated meta-analysis of advance organizer research — https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/20405 — paper — Advance organizers help, but modestly (small effects, roughly d ≈ 0.2 in the early meta-analyses). The foundation paragraph is worth keeping but is no miracle.
- [1.17] Bransford & Johnson 1972, Contextual prerequisites for understanding — https://paperguide.ai/papers/06070657-76e0-4355-b53c-5dfe9979bf2f/ — paper — Telling readers the topic before an ambiguous passage roughly doubled comprehension and recall, and telling them afterwards did not help. This is direct evidence that the foundation must come first, not as a late reveal.
- [1.18] Frederic Bartlett ("War of the Ghosts", schema) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederic_Bartlett — encyclopedia — Readers reconstruct unfamiliar material toward their existing schemas and drop or distort what does not fit. Foreign or unfamiliar concepts need explicit framing, or they will be silently converted into familiar wrong ones.
- [1.19] Refutation texts and Posner et al.'s Conceptual Change Model (Frontiers in Education, 2025) — https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2025.1472385/full — paper — Conceptual change needs four things: dissatisfaction with the old idea, and a new one that is intelligible, plausible and fruitful. A "however" station can supply exactly these.
- [1.20] The effectiveness of refutation text in confronting scientific misconceptions: a meta-analysis — https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/the-effectiveness-of-refutation-text-in-confronting-scientific-mi/fingerprints/ — paper — This pre-registered meta-analysis (76 studies, 294 effects) shows a consistent advantage for texts that state the misconception, refute it and give the alternative. It is one of the strongest single findings for adding a misconception move to sard.
- [1.21] Dedre Gentner (structure-mapping theory) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dedre_Gentner — encyclopedia — Good analogies share relational structure (systematicity), not surface features. Comparing two cases side by side helps learners extract the abstract schema.
- [1.22] Richland, Zur & Holyoak 2007, Cognitive supports for analogies in the mathematics classroom — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17525320 — paper — Analogies work when the explainer supports the mapping: a familiar source, explicit correspondences, and a visible comparison. A bare "it's like X" often fails.
- [1.23] Teaching with Analogies (Glynn, Science Teaching sourcebook) — https://www.csun.edu/~vceed002/books/sourcebook/chapters/10-analogies/teaching-analogies.html — book — The TWA model runs: introduce target, cue source, identify features, map similarities, indicate where the analogy breaks down, draw conclusions. The "breaks down" step is what prevents analogy-induced misconceptions.
- [1.24] Fyfe, McNeil, Son & Goldstone 2014, Concreteness fading in mathematics and science instruction: a systematic review — https://pc.cogs.indiana.edu/?p=885 — paper — Start concrete, then fade explicitly to the abstract. This formalizes sard's "from the fact to its meaning" and adds a requirement: the bridge to the general principle must be stated, not implied.
- [1.25] Concreteness Fading: A Method To Achieve Transfer (The Learning Scientists) — https://learningscientists.org/blog/2018/2/1-1 — blog — Concrete examples alone do not transfer, and abstractions alone are opaque. The sequence concrete → bridged → abstract is what transfers.
- [1.26] Dual-coding theory — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual-coding_theory — encyclopedia — Verbal and imagistic codes add up. For structural or process ideas, one diagram beside the narrative gives a second retrieval route. The theory is influential, but the size of its classroom effects varies.
- [1.27] Mayer's 12 Principles of Multimedia Learning — https://lucidea.com/blog/mayers-12-principles-of-multimedia-learning — blog — Coherence (cut extraneous material), signaling (cue the organization), segmenting (learner-paced chunks) and pre-training (names and traits of key concepts first) all map onto sard's structure.
- [1.28] Signaling in Training (umbrella summary of signaling meta-analyses, incl. Schneider et al. 2018) — https://qic-wd.org/node/672 — paper — Cues that highlight structure reliably help, and sard's bold connectors are signals. How the effect varies with prior knowledge is inconsistent across meta-analyses.
- [1.29] Sundararajan & Adesope 2020, Keep it coherent: seductive details meta-analysis — https://www.e-teaching.org/materialien/literatur/sundararajan-adesope-2020 — paper — Interesting but irrelevant details hurt learning (g ≈ −.33), worst when placed at the end. Colorful anecdotes and a "note of my own" that drifts off-topic are risks.
- [1.30] Test-enhanced learning: using retrieval practice to help students learn (Vanderbilt CFT, on Roediger & Karpicke 2006) — https://cft.vanderbilt.edu/?p=22452 — blog — Recalling a passage instead of rereading it raised one-week retention (about 61% vs 40% in the classic study). What follows an explanation matters as much as the explanation.
- [1.31] Cepeda et al. 2006, Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks: a review and quantitative synthesis — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/ — paper — Spacing reliably beats massing, and the best gap grows with how long you need to remember. A one-shot narrative cannot deliver this, but a sard-deck handoff can.
- [1.32] RetrievalPractice.org — https://www.retrievalpractice.org/retrievalpractice/ — blog — Low-stakes, quick retrieval prompts work in real classrooms, from K-12 to medical school. Two or three short questions are enough. No grading is needed.
- [1.33] Test first, learn later: the power of pretesting (Psychonomic Society) — https://featuredcontent.psychonomic.org/test-first-learn-later-the-power-of-pretesting-to-enhance-learning/ — blog — Trying to answer a question before the lesson improves learning of that content, even when the guess is wrong. Prequestions can narrow attention, so they should cover the main idea.
- [1.34] Bjork & Bjork 2011, Making things hard on yourself, but in a good way: desirable difficulties — https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/04/EBjork_RBjork_2011.pdf — paper — Fluent, easy processing feels like learning but often is not. A smooth narrative risks a fluency illusion unless the reader is made to do some retrieval or generation.
- [1.35] Illusion of explanatory depth (Rozenblit & Keil 2002) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusion_of_explanatory_depth — encyclopedia — People overrate their grasp of explanatory knowledge more than of facts or narratives, and the illusion collapses when they try to explain. Asking readers to explain back corrects it.
- [1.36] Curse of knowledge — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curse_of_knowledge — encyclopedia — Experts cannot easily simulate not knowing. In the tapping study, tappers predicted 50% recognition and got 2.5%. An LLM explainer is the extreme case, so sard needs an explicit "skipped step" audit.
- [1.37] The Feynman Technique: Learning Through Explanation — https://thefiltered.substack.com/p/feynman-technique — blog — The technique is popular but has not been directly tested. Its value comes from self-explanation, retrieval and gap detection, so use it as a closing "explain it simply in your own words" prompt and do not claim special evidence for it.
- [1.38] Revised Bloom's Taxonomy (Iowa State CELT) — https://www.celt.iastate.edu/teaching/effective-practice/revised-blooms-taxonomy/ — docs — Two axes: process (remember → create) and knowledge type (factual, conceptual, procedural, metacognitive). Core sard targets "understand / conceptual", so other goals (apply, evaluate, procedural) need different moves.
- [1.39] SOLO taxonomy description (CUHK) — https://www.cuhk.edu.hk/policy/assessment/SOLO-description.pdf — docs — Multistructural means several facts that are unconnected (what bullet lists produce). Relational means integrated into a coherent whole (what sard aims at). Extended abstract means transferred to new contexts (what sard rarely reaches).
- [1.40] Threshold Concepts Guide (University of Calgary, Taylor Institute) — https://taylorinstitute.ucalgary.ca/sites/default/files/TI%20Guides/Threshold_Concepts_Guide.pdf — docs — Some concepts are troublesome, transformative and integrative "portals". An explanation should find the one concept the whole chain hinges on and spend most of its effort there.
- [1.41] Jerome Bruner on the scaffolding of learning — https://www.teachthought.com/learning/jerome-bruner/ — blog — Scaffolding (Wood, Bruner & Ross 1976) is temporary support within the learner's zone of proximal development, faded as competence grows. It argues for a sard that hands more work to the reader over a session.
- [1.42] A brief guide to Rosenshine's 10 Principles of Instruction — https://www.innerdrive.co.uk/blog/guide-rosenshine-10-principles/ — blog — Effective teachers present new material in small steps and check understanding at each point. sard has the small steps but no checks.
- [1.43] The Science of Learning (Deans for Impact, 2nd ed.) — https://www.deansforimpact.org/files/assets/thescienceoflearning2nded.pdf — docs — A compact consensus: new ideas are understood through what learners already know, abstract ideas need concrete examples, and retrieval and spacing aid retention. Its misconception list (e.g. learning styles) is a guard against unsupported features.
- [1.44] Mar, Li, Nguyen & Ta 2021, Memory and comprehension of narrative versus expository texts: a meta-analysis — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8219577/ — paper — Across more than 75 samples and 33,000 participants, narrative beat expository text (overall g = .55, memory .72, comprehension .48). This supports sard's core, with caveats: some publication bias and only four delayed-test studies.
- [1.45] Ask the Cognitive Scientist: The Privileged Status of Story (Willingham 2004) — https://www.aft.org/periodical/american-educator/summer-2004/ask-cognitive-scientist — news — Willingham's "four Cs" are causality, conflict, complications and character. Causality and conflict are the parts sard can borrow without fictionalizing. He advises using story elements where full stories are inappropriate.
- [1.46] Role of Causal Relationships in the Story Comprehension of Children (causal-network line, Trabasso and colleagues) — https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/72079 — paper — Statements on the causal chain are recalled more and judged more important than "dead ends". This is an empirical basis for sard's "no hanging station" rule.
- [1.47] Comprehension Effects of Connectives Across Texts, Readers, and Coherence Relations — https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/comprehension-effects-of-connectives-across-texts-readers-and-coh/ — paper — Connectives help mostly locally and mainly in difficult texts. The research line reports that causal and contrastive connectives aid comprehension, while additive ones ("also") can impede it. Connector type matters, not just presence.
- [1.48] Sanders & Noordman 2000, The role of coherence relations and their linguistic markers in text processing — https://repository.tilburguniversity.edu/items/35f9dadc-ca9c-4904-b962-3fcdd68cd1ba — paper — Explicit relation markers sped processing but did not improve recall. Connectors are processing instructions, not memory boosters on their own.
- [1.49] Williams & Lombrozo 2010, The role of explanation in discovery and generalization — https://cognition.princeton.edu/sites/default/files/cognition/files/williams_et_al-2010-cognitive_science.pdf — paper — Explaining pushes learners toward broad unifying patterns and better generalization. This supports asking the reader to explain, and stating the general principle at the "arrival".
- [1.50] Lombrozo 2007, Simplicity and probability in causal explanation — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17097080/ — paper — People over-prefer simpler explanations (fewer causes) and demand disproportionate evidence before accepting complex ones. A tidy single-chain narrative can feel more true than it is.
- [1.51] Deconstructing the seductive allure of neuroscience explanations (Judgment and Decision Making, 2015) — https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/judgment-and-decision-making/article/deconstructing-the-seductive-allure-of-neuroscience-explanations/568C206CD761E70374975276BBF69737 — paper — This follows Weisberg et al. 2008: irrelevant technical detail makes weak explanations feel more satisfying to non-experts. Satisfaction is not evidence of a good explanation, so sard's honesty rules carry real weight.

## Findings
**Explanations fail by default, and the failure is predictable.** Wittwer and Renkl's framework [1.1] says instructional explanations often produce little learning for two reasons. The learner stays mentally passive, and the explanation does not address that learner's specific gap. ICAP [1.4] gives the mechanism: reading is passive or active, and gains come from constructive or interactive engagement. Roelle-style experiments [1.2] show the fix is concrete. Withhold one inference and prompt the reader to generate it, then follow up with a remedial explanation and a "what did you revise?" prompt. Today sard is a polished one-way monologue that ends with "next chapter?". By this evidence it optimizes the explanation and neglects the learner's processing of it.

**Prior knowledge is the dominant moderator, so one depth cannot fit all.** The expertise-reversal effect [1.11], the reverse cohesion effect [1.12] and the guidance literature [1.13] converge. Explicit foundations, fully marked connectors and concrete steps help novices, but can be redundant or even harmful for knowledgeable readers, who learn more when left some inferences. Tailoring works even from thin diagnostic signals [1.3]. sard currently has one register.

**The foundation-first design is well supported, but its effect size is modest and its form matters.** Topic-before-text roughly doubles comprehension compared with topic-after [1.17]. Schemas reshape unfamiliar material toward the familiar [1.18], and naming key concepts first helps (pre-training [1.27]). Advance-organizer meta-analyses show small effects [1.16], and Ausubel insists the organizer sit at a higher level of generality than the content, not act as a summary [1.15]. sard's "foundation" fits that. The "compass" ("where we will arrive") is closer to an overview, the weaker variant.

**Narrative's advantage is real, and its active ingredient is causal connectivity.** Mar et al. [1.44] find narrative texts better remembered (g ≈ .72) and understood (g ≈ .48) than expository ones. Willingham [1.45] attributes this mainly to causality and conflict. Causal-network studies [1.46] show that what sits on the causal chain is remembered while dead ends are lost. That validates sard's "no hanging station" rule and its insistence on true connectors. One caveat: the meta-analysis compares stories with essays, not expository content retold in connected prose (sard's actual move). The safest claim is "causally connected prose", not "storytelling".

**Connectors are signals, and type matters more than count.** Signaling reliably helps [1.28], so bold connectors are a sound device. Relation markers speed processing but do not by themselves raise recall [1.48], and their effects are mostly local and strongest in difficult texts [1.47]. The same research line reports that causal and contrastive connectives help comprehension while additive ones can hinder it. sard's honest-connector rule is right. The next step is to rank connectors: causal, contrastive and inferential ones carry understanding, while "then / after that" should be used only for genuine chronology.

**Misconceptions need a dedicated move, and narrative has a natural slot for it.** Refutation texts (state the common belief, refute it, give a plausible alternative) consistently beat plain exposition [1.20]. That matches Posner's conditions for conceptual change [1.19]. Wittwer and Renkl's "marked explanations", which name the flaw before revising it [1.1], are the same idea. sard has a "however / the paradox is" connector but no rule to look for the reader's likely wrong reading. A refutation station is the most evidence-dense addition available.

**Analogies and examples need discipline.** Analogies teach through shared relations, not surface features [1.21]. They work when the source is familiar and the mapping is supported [1.22], and they must say where they break [1.23]. Abstract points need concrete instances, faded explicitly to the general principle [1.24][1.25][1.43]. sard lists "for example / imagine" as a connector type but gives no quality rules. For procedures, the worked example narrated with reasons is the evidence-backed form [1.8][1.9][1.10].

**What follows the explanation determines what lasts.** Retrieval beats rereading for retention [1.30][1.32], spacing beats massing [1.31], and prequestions before a lesson aid learning [1.33]. Smooth, fluent material creates an illusion of competence [1.34], and people especially overrate explanatory knowledge until asked to produce it [1.35]. A beautiful sard narrative read once is exactly the kind of fluent input that inflates the feeling of understanding. Self-explanation (g ≈ .55) [1.5] and elaborative why-questions [1.6] are cheap ways to turn the ending into constructive activity. Feynman-style "explain it simply" fits, though its evidence is borrowed from these mechanisms, not direct [1.37].

**Understanding has levels, and sard implicitly targets only one.** SOLO separates disconnected facts (multistructural, what bullet lists produce) from integrated structure (relational) and transfer (extended abstract) [1.39]. Revised Bloom adds process and knowledge-type axes [1.38]. sard's narrative is a strong engine for the relational level of conceptual knowledge. It has no explicit move for transfer (apply to a new case), for evaluation (sard-article partly covers this), or for procedural knowledge. Threshold-concept work [1.40] suggests that in most topics one troublesome concept carries the whole chain and deserves most of the space.

**The explainer's own biases are a design risk.** The curse of knowledge [1.36] predicts experts skip steps they cannot see, and an LLM is the extreme expert. People also prefer simpler, single-chain explanations beyond what the evidence warrants [1.50], and irrelevant technical detail makes weak explanations feel satisfying [1.51]. A narrative engine can produce explanations that are coherent, satisfying and wrong. sard's rules against inventing context and for true connectors are its main defense and should be strengthened, not relaxed.

**Chunking and pacing.** Small steps with checks [1.42], learner-paced segments [1.27] and scaffolding that fades [1.41] point the same way. For long sources (book chapters, lectures), delivering segments with a short check between them is better supported than a single long file the reader skims once.

## Gaps in sard today
- No calibration to the reader's prior knowledge. The same foundation and connector density go to novices and experts (expertise reversal, reverse cohesion) [1.11][1.12][1.3].
- No misconception or refutation move. sard never asks "what will this reader probably get wrong?" [1.19][1.20][1.1].
- The output is purely passive. No constructive step, no withheld inference, no explain-back. The closing line offers more content, not processing [1.2][1.4][1.5].
- Nothing comes after the explanation: no retrieval check, no spacing hook (sard-deck exists but is not linked to the core flow) [1.30][1.31][1.32].
- There are no quality rules for analogies (relational mapping, familiar source, where it breaks) or for concrete examples and non-examples [1.21][1.22][1.23][1.24].
- Connectors are typed by function but not ranked by explanatory power. Additive or sequential links can pass as "honest" while carrying no logic [1.47][1.48].
- The compass is a destination summary, not a higher-level organizer or a driving question [1.15][1.33].
- There is no curse-of-knowledge audit in the pre-send check (skipped steps, undefined terms inside stations) [1.36].
- There is no guard against "too tidy" narratives on multi-causal or contested topics, and nowhere to say "the source does not establish this link" [1.50][1.51].
- There is no procedural mode (worked example with reasons, then a faded example) [1.8][1.9][1.10].
- There is no explicit target level of understanding (relational vs transfer vs evaluation), and no identification of the threshold concept [1.38][1.39][1.40].
- Long material is handled by "write a file", not by segmenting with checks [1.27][1.42].

## Proposals for sard
- P1.1: **Reader calibration step.** Before writing, infer the reader's level from the request, history and phrasing. If it is unclear and the topic is technical, ask one short question or state the level being assumed. Novice: full foundation, explicit causal connectors, a concrete example in most stations. Knowledgeable: compressed foundation, fewer marked links, more left to inference. (sources: 1.3, 1.11, 1.12, 1.13) This keeps the narrative and only tunes its density, which is the evidence-backed way to stay right for both audiences.
- P1.2: **Connector hierarchy rule.** Rank connectors: causal, inferential and contrastive ("that's why / it follows / however") carry the chain, and sequential ("then / after that") is allowed only for real chronology. Add a test: if two stations can swap order without breaking anything, the chain is not causal yet, so rework it. (sources: 1.46, 1.47, 1.48, 1.45) This sharpens sard's existing honest-connector rule toward the ingredient (causal connectivity) that makes narrative work.
- P1.3: **Optional "misconception station" (refutation move).** When a topic has a common wrong reading, include one station that states it fairly, shows why it fails, and offers the alternative as intelligible and plausible. It opens with a contrastive connector ("It is tempting to read this as X; however..."). (sources: 1.19, 1.20, 1.1, 1.18) It fits the narrative core as the "however" turn and is the single best-evidenced addition.
- P1.4: **Foundation as true organizer, compass as driving question.** The foundation states the frame one level more general than the content and names the 2–4 key terms (pre-training). The optional compass becomes the question or problem the idea answers, not a preview of the conclusion. (sources: 1.15, 1.16, 1.17, 1.27, 1.33, 1.14) This turns the opening into narrative tension (Willingham's "conflict") that the arrival resolves.
- P1.5: **Analogy and example rules.** Analogies must map relations, use a source familiar to this reader, and end with one clause on where they break. Abstract stations get one concrete case and then an explicit bridge sentence to the general principle (concreteness fading). Add a non-example when the boundary of a concept matters. (sources: 1.21, 1.22, 1.23, 1.24, 1.25) This makes "from the fact to its meaning" operational instead of stylistic.
- P1.6: **"Check your path" ending (replaces or precedes the next-step line).** Offer 2–3 why/how questions that can only be answered by following the chain, not by locating a sentence. Alternatively, invite the reader to retell the idea in 3–4 sentences. Keep answers out of the main text, available on request. (sources: 1.5, 1.6, 1.30, 1.32, 1.35, 1.37) This turns a passive read into constructive engagement at the cost of a few lines.
- P1.7: **Explain-back feedback loop (`/sard check` or a natural follow-up).** When the reader retells or answers, diagnose the specific broken link and re-narrate only that station as a marked explanation ("You connected A to C directly; the missing step is B, because..."), then ask what they revised. (sources: 1.1, 1.2, 1.3, 1.4) This is the interactive tier of ICAP while staying narrative: it repairs one station of the chain.
- P1.8: **Optional "one gap" mode for motivated learners.** Withhold one inference link (marked "pause: what follows from this?"), let the reader attempt it, then reveal it. Default it off, and offer it for study contexts. (sources: 1.2, 1.4, 1.33, 1.34) It creates a desirable difficulty inside the narrative without breaking its flow.
- P1.9: **Curse-of-knowledge and fluency audit in the pre-send check.** Add: "Does any station rely on a term or step not given in the foundation? Could a reader at the calibrated level rebuild the chain from the bold connectors alone? Did I add any vivid detail that does not serve the chain?" (sources: 1.36, 1.29, 1.7, 1.12) This protects the narrative's coherence from the explainer's blind spots and from seductive details.
- P1.10: **Coherence honesty clause.** When the source does not establish a causal link, say so ("the author asserts this without explaining why"). On multi-causal or contested topics, narrate the competing accounts as a fork instead of forcing one chain. Never add technical color to make a weak link feel strong. (sources: 1.50, 1.51, 1.29) This keeps sard's persuasive power aligned with truth, the reason the connector rule exists.
- P1.11: **Threshold-concept spotlight.** In preparation, name the one concept the whole chain hinges on. Give it the foundation or its own station, and say plainly that this is the hinge. (sources: 1.40, 1.38) It concentrates explanatory effort where understanding actually turns, which is a narrative climax rather than an extra section.
- P1.12: **Procedural mode (worked-example narration).** For math, code or method content, narrate one worked example where every step opens with its reason ("because..."), then give a second example with the final steps left to the reader. (sources: 1.8, 1.9, 1.10, 1.13) This extends sard to procedural knowledge using the same connector logic.
- P1.13: **Transfer at the arrival.** After "the message in brief", optionally add one sentence applying the idea to a new case outside the source (marked as the explainer's, like "a note of my own"). (sources: 1.39, 1.49, 1.25) This pushes from relational toward extended-abstract understanding without adding a new section type.
- P1.14: **Segment long material with checks.** For lectures and chapters above a threshold, deliver in parts (each a complete mini-narrative), with one retrieval question between parts and the user controlling pace. The file option stays for archiving. (sources: 1.27, 1.42, 1.41) Each segment is still a full narrative with its own arrival.
- P1.15: **Spacing handoff to sard-deck.** At the end of a study-oriented sard, offer to turn the stations and the check questions into a spaced review deck. sard-deck already exists, so this is a link, not a feature. (sources: 1.31, 1.30, 1.6) Durable retention needs spacing, which a single narrative cannot supply.
- P1.16: **One diagram, only when the idea is structural.** For processes, systems or causal loops, add one simple diagram of the mechanism or the station chain. No decorative images. (sources: 1.26, 1.27, 1.29) It complements the narrative with a second code without diluting it.

## Cautions
- **Over-scaffolding experts.** Making the full structure mandatory (compass, foundation, 7 stations, checks) contradicts the expertise-reversal and reverse-cohesion evidence [1.11][1.12]. Calibration must be able to remove scaffolding, not only add it.
- **The narrative-advantage evidence is narrower than the slogan.** Mar et al. [1.44] compare stories with essays, show some publication bias, and have few delayed tests. Willingham [1.45] is a reasoned synthesis, not a trial. No study cited here tests sard's specific "expository content as connected prose" format against well-structured exposition. Claim causal connectivity as the active ingredient, not "stories teach better".
- **Coherence can mislead.** People over-prefer simple single-cause explanations [1.50], and irrelevant detail inflates satisfaction with bad explanations [1.51]. A fluent narrative raises confidence more than understanding [1.34][1.35]. The honest-connector rule and the "don't invent context" rule are the safety system. Any redesign that loosens them for flow is harmful.
- **Connectors are not magic.** Explicit markers speed reading but do not by themselves improve recall [1.48], and their benefit is mostly local [1.47]. A connector on every sentence becomes noise. Keep the bold connector at station level.
- **Seductive storytelling.** Vivid anecdotes, dramatic framing and an off-topic "note of my own" can lower learning (g ≈ −.33) [1.29]. Story elements must serve the causal chain.
- **Do not turn sard into a quiz engine.** Retrieval and self-explanation are strongly supported [1.5][1.30], but stacking long quizzes onto every answer would bury the narrative and break the owner's "match length to request" rule. Keep checks at 2–3 lines, optional, and off for quick questions.
- **Productive failure is contested.** Its benefits depend on prior knowledge and careful design [1.14], and guided-instruction research warns against unguided discovery for novices [1.13]. Use it only as a framing question, never as "figure it out yourself".
- **Prose-only is not always right.** For genuinely parallel or reference content (multistructural by nature: a list of conditions, a comparison table), lists and tables are effective signals [1.28]. Keep sard's exception for structures the author enumerates, and allow a compact table as a supplement after the narrative when the reader will look things up.
- **Avoid unsupported "learner type" features.** Do not add "visual/auditory learner" modes. Learning-styles matching is a documented myth [1.43]. The diagram proposal (P1.16) is justified by content structure, not by learner type.
- **Feynman technique branding.** Its evidence is indirect, borrowed from self-explanation and retrieval [1.37]. Implement the mechanism and do not market it as a validated method.
